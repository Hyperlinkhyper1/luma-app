# 3D FLUID SIMULATION BENCHMARK — WATER IN A GLASS TANK

Build a real-time, interactive 3D fluid simulation as a single HTML page:
water sloshing in a glass tank that the user can stir, tilt and drop
objects into.

This is a physics, GPU-programming and rendering benchmark. Every model
gets the same brief. It must be a genuine three-dimensional simulation of
an incompressible liquid — NOT a 2D simulation drawn in 3D, NOT a
height-field of waves on a flat surface, NOT pre-baked animation.

==================================================
LIBRARIES
==================================================

Use Three.js r160, loaded with exactly this importmap:

```html
<script type="importmap">
{"imports":{"three":"https://unpkg.com/three@0.160.0/build/three.module.js","three/addons/":"https://unpkg.com/three@0.160.0/examples/jsm/"}}
</script>
```

Official Three.js addons (OrbitControls, PointerLockControls,
EffectComposer, UnrealBloomPass, Sky, Water, RoomEnvironment, ...) may be
imported from "three/addons/". Nothing else may be loaded from the network:
no external models (GLTF/OBJ/FBX), textures, images, HDRIs, fonts, audio
files or other libraries. Every mesh, texture and sound is generated by your
code (procedural geometry, CanvasTexture / DataTexture, shaders, Web Audio).

CSS goes inside <style>. JavaScript goes inside <script type="module">.
You may also use raw WebGL2 for the simulation itself.

==================================================
THE SIMULATION
==================================================

Choose and implement a real method, and name it in the UI:

- SPH or Position Based Fluids (particles), or
- FLIP / PIC / APIC (particles plus a grid with a pressure solve), or
- another established 3D method.

Run it on the GPU (WebGL2 float render targets, ping-pong textures and
fragment-shader passes) wherever possible. Check for float render target
support (EXT_color_buffer_float); if it is missing, fall back to a CPU
solver with fewer particles and say so on screen.

Targets:

- at least 20,000 particles on the GPU path (more is better), with the
  count adjustable; show the current count
- incompressibility: the liquid keeps its volume — it does not compress
  into a puddle or slowly gain or lose volume; show the average density
  error
- stability: it never explodes, jitters at rest or leaks through the
  walls, even when the tank is shaken hard
- correct boundaries: the liquid collides with the tank walls and floor and
  with solid objects, with a visible splash and settling

==================================================
SCENARIOS
==================================================

Provide a scenario picker (each resets the simulation):

1. DAM BREAK (default): a column of water collapses across the tank
2. DOUBLE DAM: two columns collide in the middle
3. DROP: a sphere of water falls into a shallow pool
4. FAUCET: a continuous stream pours in from above (cap the particle count)
5. WAVE MACHINE: one wall moves back and forth and makes waves

==================================================
INTERACTION
==================================================

- Click and drag in the water to push it (a ray-cast stirring force or a
  moving solid sphere).
- Tilt the tank with the arrow keys or a slider; gravity follows the
  tank's orientation and the water sloshes realistically.
- DROP OBJECT: a solid object (ball, crate or rubber duck) falls in,
  splashes and floats according to its density. Two-way coupling (the
  object moves the water and the water moves the object) is a bonus.
- Orbit, zoom and pan the camera around the tank.

Controls: viscosity, particle count / grid resolution, gravity strength,
time step, pause, single step, reset. Stats overlay: fps, particle count,
simulation time per frame in ms.

==================================================
RENDERING
==================================================

Render the liquid as liquid, not as dots by default:

- preferably screen-space fluid rendering (particle depth, bilateral
  smoothing, reconstructed normals, refraction of what is behind the water,
  Fresnel reflection, light absorption tinted by thickness), or a
  marching-cubes / ray-marched surface
- a toggle to see the raw particles coloured by speed
- foam or spray for fast, splashing particles is a bonus

The scene around it: a glass tank with visible edges and reflections on a
lab bench or table, soft lighting and shadows. Caustics on the tank floor
are a bonus.

==================================================
QUALITY BAR (APPLIES TO EVERYTHING ABOVE)
==================================================

- No placeholders, TODOs, "coming soon" buttons or dead controls. Every
  control on screen does what its label says.
- The scene starts on its own within a few seconds of opening, with a short
  loading state if generation takes time — never a page stuck on "Loading".
- It fills the window and handles resizes. Clamp the device pixel ratio
  (at most 2) so it stays smooth on high-DPI screens.
- Aim for a steady 60 fps on a mid-range laptop GPU. Use instancing, merged
  geometry and level of detail where the object count is high.
- If WebGL is unavailable, show a clear message instead of a blank page.
- Keep the UI compact and legible over the 3D view; it must not cover the
  subject of the scene. Provide a way to hide it.
- Prefer real shapes over boxes: curves, bevels, lathed and extruded
  profiles, custom BufferGeometry. A scene made of plain cubes fails.
